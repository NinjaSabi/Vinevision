Communication · Leadership

The Conversation You Keep Avoiding Is the One You Need Most

Nicole Marshall · 5 min read

There's a conversation happening in your organisation right now — except it isn't actually happening. It's happening in the car park, in WhatsApp groups, in the careful silences after a team meeting. It's the thing everyone knows but nobody's saying out loud.

In over 15 years of working with organisations across South Africa, I've learned that the biggest performance problems are almost never strategy problems, or skill problems, or even resource problems. They're conversation problems. Somebody somewhere is not saying something that needs to be said.

What a Crucial Conversation Actually Is

The term comes from the research behind the Crucial Learning® programme — a body of work that examined what distinguishes organisations that perform well over time from those that struggle. The finding was consistent: the highest-performing teams and organisations had developed the capacity to hold difficult conversations well. The lowest-performing ones hadn't.

A crucial conversation is any interaction where three conditions are present: opinions differ, stakes are high, and emotions run strong. These are the moments when most people either go silent or go aggressive — both of which make things worse.

The silent response is more common than most leaders realise. It looks like nodding along in a meeting and then complaining about the decision afterwards. It looks like a manager who avoids giving honest feedback because they're afraid of conflict. It looks like a team that agrees publicly and disagrees privately — a pattern that creates the illusion of alignment while actually producing resentment and disengagement.

The Cost of Silence

Avoiding a crucial conversation is never free, even when it feels like the safe option. Every time a conversation doesn't happen, a few things occur. The problem doesn't go away — it often gets worse. The relationship weakens slightly, because authentic connection requires honesty. And a signal gets sent to everyone watching: that certain things can't be talked about here. That signal, repeated over time, becomes culture.

I worked with a leadership team once where a particular senior leader had been significantly underperforming for nearly two years. Everyone knew it — the CEO, the other executives, the team members who reported to him. The conversations about him happened everywhere except with him. By the time the situation was finally addressed, the damage to team morale, the company's results and the individual himself was enormous. The kindest thing anyone could have done, 18 months earlier, was to say something directly.

Why We Avoid Them

The brain treats social threat the same way it treats physical threat. When a conversation feels risky — when we fear rejection, conflict, damaging a relationship or being wrong — the threat response activates, and we default to whatever feels safest. For most people, that means either withdrawing (silence) or attacking (aggression).

The trouble is that neither response actually resolves anything. Silence accumulates into resentment. Aggression produces compliance at best and rebellion at worst. Neither builds the trust and understanding needed for a real solution.

What's needed is a third path: a way of entering difficult conversations that holds both honesty and safety simultaneously. This is a learnable skill — but it requires deliberate practice and often a new set of mental models about what these conversations are actually for.

Three Questions to Identify Your Crucial Conversations

Here's a simple diagnostic you can do right now. Think about your organisation, your team, or your most important relationships at work. Then ask these three questions.

First: what's the problem that keeps coming back? If the same issue resurfaces in meetings, in performance reviews, in exit interviews — that's a signal that the conversation addressing the root cause hasn't happened yet.

Second: what do people talk about privately but not publicly? The gap between what's said in the room and what's said in the car park is where your most important conversations live. That gap represents unspent trust and avoided honesty.

Third: who on your team or in your leadership group is getting away with something? Not held accountable for behaviour that everyone has noticed? The reason is almost always that nobody has been willing to have that conversation — and the cost of that silence is paid by everyone around them.

What Happens When You Get It Right

When organisations build the capacity to hold crucial conversations well, the results are measurable and significant. Teams that can talk about hard things without destroying relationships see faster decision-making, higher trust, lower turnover and better performance. Not because conflict disappears — but because it gets resolved productively instead of festering underground.

The skills aren't complicated. But they do need to be practised. And they work best when there's a shared language and a shared set of tools across a team — so that the conversation about the conversation is already half-done before it starts.

The conversation you keep avoiding is costing you more than you think. And it's usually far less catastrophic to have than you fear.
