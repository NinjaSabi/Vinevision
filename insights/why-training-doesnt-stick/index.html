Why Most Training Doesn't Stick — And What To Do About It

Nicole Marshall · 6 min read

Walk into most organisations and you'll find a graveyard of training initiatives. The workshop was great. People were engaged. The feedback forms came back glowing. And then... nothing changed.

This is one of the most common and costly problems in people development — and it has a name. Learning professionals call it the transfer problem: the gap between what people learn in the room and what they actually do on Monday morning.

The Myth of Good Content

Here's the uncomfortable truth: the quality of the content is rarely the problem. Most training fails not because it's poorly designed, but because organisations treat learning as an event rather than a process.

Think about how most training works. People are pulled out of their day, taken to a room (or a Zoom call), exposed to new ideas and frameworks, and then sent back to an environment that hasn't changed at all. Their manager hasn't been briefed. Their KPIs still reward the same old behaviours. Nobody ever follows up. Within a week, the old habits reassert themselves — because habits are context-dependent, and the context hasn't shifted.

Research consistently shows that without reinforcement, most new skills begin to fade within 72 hours of training. Within a month, the majority of what was learned is functionally gone.

What Actually Drives Transfer

There are three conditions that make learning stick. In over 15 years of L&D work, I've found these to be non-negotiable.

Manager involvement. The single biggest predictor of whether training sticks is whether the learner's direct manager actively supports application. Not just "encourages" it in a vague sense — but creates specific opportunities to practise, acknowledges effort, and holds people accountable for using what they've learned. When managers aren't briefed and involved, the training is essentially working against the culture rather than with it.

Practice opportunities. New skills need low-stakes rehearsal before they're applied in high-pressure situations. Training that sends people back to the most difficult conversations of their career immediately after learning a new framework is setting them up to fail. The skill needs to be practised in safer conditions first — in team meetings, in 1:1s, in role plays — until it starts to feel natural.

Environmental redesign. Often, the old behaviour is the path of least resistance. If you want people to do something differently, you need to make the new behaviour easier and the old behaviour harder. This might mean changing meeting structures, adjusting performance conversations, or redesigning workflows. The learning can't do that work on its own.

What This Means for How You Design Learning

If you want training to create lasting change, start thinking of it as a 70-day initiative, not a one-day event. The workshop itself is maybe 10% of the work. The rest is what happens before and after.

Before the programme, brief managers on what their teams will be learning and what their role is in supporting transfer. Create psychological safety for people to try new things and sometimes get them wrong. Establish a baseline — what does current behaviour look like, and what would improvement actually look like in measurable terms?

After the programme, build in structured practice sessions. Create peer accountability pairs. Schedule deliberate follow-up conversations between managers and team members specifically about applying the learning. And measure outputs, not just inputs — not "did they attend?" but "what changed?"

The Honest Conversation Organisations Need to Have

Sometimes, when I'm working with an organisation on their L&D strategy, the most important thing I can do is point out that they don't actually have a training problem. They have a culture problem, or a management problem, or a clarity problem. And sending people to training without addressing those underlying issues is expensive theatre.

A good diagnostic will always reveal whether the issue is a skill gap or a will gap — whether people don't know how to do something differently, or whether they know how but aren't motivated or enabled to. The interventions for those two situations are completely different. Training addresses the first. Culture change, incentive redesign and leadership modelling address the second.

The organisations that get the best return from their L&D investment are the ones willing to have that honest conversation first — and then design interventions that address the actual problem, not just the visible symptom.
